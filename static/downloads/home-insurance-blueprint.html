Summary

For most homeowners the price is not the biggest risk. The gaps are. The largest ways a home policy fails a household are too little house cover to rebuild, a percentage storm deductible or actual-cash-value roof that quietly shifts thousands to the owner, exclusions (flood, sewer backup, code upgrades) nobody knew about, and losing cover at renewal. The home module of the checker was rebuilt around those four risks, with price as one part.

  • Rebuild cost is the biggest single exposure and the data disagrees. Published estimates run from $162 a square foot (NAHB, new construction) to $280 (NerdWallet with First Street data) to $350 (an Insure.com survey of actual rebuilds). The first version of the tool assumed $200, which would have called most homes adequately insured. It now shows all three.
  • Storm deductibles and roof terms decide what a storm claim really pays. A 2% hurricane deductible on a $560,000 house is $11,200 before the insurer pays anything; the average wind or hail claim is about $13,500. An older roof paid at actual cash value can leave most of a $20,000 replacement to the owner.
  • A policy stress test now shows, for the user's own answers, what the policy would pay in a fire, total loss, wind or hail, hurricane, roof replacement, burst pipe, sewer backup, theft, small and large liability suits, flood and earthquake.
  • Home prices are still the weakest number. They start from one published dataset (Insurance.com, September 15, 2026), and a second published source is 25% to 60% lower in the ten states compared. The claims and credit effects are now sourced; roof, age and construction effects are still assumptions.
  • 1,737 automated checks pass on the rebuilt checker (in a simulated browser). The model was also tested against the Oklahoma Insurance Department's official per-insurer home premiums (see section 3); no real quotes for the pilot states were available.

All dollar figures for the user's own home come from a learning model, not a quote. Published averages are cited with their source and date.

1. Market conditions

  • Claim costs are at records. LexisNexis reports all-peril claim severity in 2025 was up 25.9% from 2024 and 93.2% above 2019, even though claim frequency fell 23.8%; loss cost was still about 50% above 2019. Fire and lightning severity rose 67.3% in 2025, largely from the Los Angeles wildfires (estimated at $61.2 billion). In 2024, catastrophe claims were 42% of claims and 64% of losses.
  • Price pressure shows up in state averages. Insurance.com's 2026 data found the largest increases in Louisiana (58%), Michigan (48%), Virginia (37%), Kentucky (33%) and Minnesota (29%).
  • Access to cover is tightening in some states. California's FAIR Plan, the insurer of last resort, held about 668,000 policies with about $724 billion of exposure at a January 2026 legislative hearing, and its president attributed slower growth to fewer bulk non-renewals. Florida's Citizens plays the same role. See section 9.
  • Consequence for a consumer tool: availability and coverage terms matter as much as price. A cheap quote from a market that later non-renews you, or one that pays the roof at depreciated value, is not a bargain.

2. How a homeowners policy works

Most owner-occupied policies are HO-3 forms. The structure below comes from state-run insurer (Florida Citizens) coverage worksheets for private carriers and other filed programs; details vary by insurer and state.

CoverageWhat it coversTypical terms
A. DwellingThe house. All causes of loss except exclusions.Replacement cost. Some forms apply a coinsurance penalty if the house is insured for less than 80% of replacement cost.
B. Other structuresDetached garage, fence, shed.2% to 10% of Coverage A by default in the worksheets reviewed; higher limits available.
C. Personal propertyContents. Named perils.Often paid at actual cash value; replacement cost is an add-on. Included at about 25% to 50% of Coverage A.
D. Loss of useExtra living costs if the home is unlivable.About 10% to 20% of Coverage A.
E. Personal liabilityLawsuits for injury or damage you cause.Options such as $100,000 to $500,000; no deductible.
F. Medical payments to othersSmall medical bills for guests.$2,000 to $5,000.

Special limits inside Coverage C

Worksheets reviewed show money capped at $200, securities at $1,000 to $1,500, jewelry and furs (theft) at $1,000 to $1,500, firearms (theft) at $1,000 to $2,500, silverware (theft) at $2,500, and business property at $2,500 on the premises and $250 off it. Valuables above these limits need a scheduled endorsement.

Endorsements that change outcomes

  • Ordinance or law: pays to bring a rebuilt home up to current codes. A common example is 10% of Coverage A.
  • Extended replacement cost: adds roughly 10% to 50% of the dwelling limit if rebuilding costs more.
  • Water backup: sewer, drain and sump backup, often around $10,000 as an add-on. Usually excluded from a standard policy.
  • Service line and equipment breakdown: underground utilities and appliance or HVAC breakdown, offered as add-ons by some insurers.
  • Replacement cost on contents, and an umbrella policy for liability above the home limit.

This is one state at one time and standard programs only. The tidy data is in oklahoma-official-insurer-samples.csv and the full write-up is in the main report.

How much the sources disagree

An InsuranceQuotes/Quadrant table gives $300,000 averages that are 38% to 80% of the Insurance.com figures in ten states: Florida $4,218 vs. $8,471, Texas $3,420 vs. $4,582, California $1,326 vs. $1,653, New York $1,234 vs. $1,844, Ohio $1,020 vs. $2,109, Oklahoma $3,645 vs. $5,378, Georgia $1,489 vs. $2,301, Illinois $1,273 vs. $2,802, Colorado $2,115 vs. $5,511, Louisiana $3,930 vs. $5,185. A Compare.com table lists Florida at $10,675, and national averages across sources run from about $1,700 to $2,872. The earlier plus or minus 35% range (now x0.73 to x1.37) contained the other source's figure in only 5 of the 10 states. Definitions, dates and home assumptions differ, so no home figure should be read as exact.

Insurance.com's earlier vintage moved by at most 17% to the September 2026 table across 17 states compared, which is why the range is wide but not arbitrary.

A University of Colorado study of nearly 5,000 affected policyholders found homeowners were underinsured by an average of $139,000, and that many buy the amount their insurer suggests. Smaller homes cost more per square foot because fixed costs weigh more.

Design decision: the tool shows the three estimates side by side with the user's limit as a share of each, the dollar shortfall, and the coinsurance warning below 80% of the midpoint, and it tells the user to get a contractor or insurer estimate. It never treats one figure as the truth. Its earlier fixed assumption of $200 a square foot has been removed.

5. Storm deductibles

  • Hurricane and wind or hail deductibles are usually a percentage of the dwelling limit, not of the claim. Typical hurricane deductibles run 1% to 5% and can reach 10% in high-risk areas. Some insurers offer flat dollar amounts, which are becoming less common in high-risk zones.
  • Nineteen states and Washington, D.C. have some form of hurricane, named-storm or windstorm deductible (Policygenius, 2024).
  • Florida sets the rules by statute: insurers must offer $500, 2%, 5% and 10% options; the deductible applies once per hurricane season; and it is triggered only by a hurricane declared by the National Weather Service. The same rules apply at Citizens.
  • North Carolina's Department of Insurance describes a named-storm deductible as a percentage of Coverage A, giving a 2% deductible on $300,000 as $6,000.

6. Roof age and payment terms

  • Sources agree that roof age around 15 years is where insurers begin to ask for inspections, shift the roof to actual cash value, add surcharges, or decline new business; by 20 years many restrict cover or decline. Most of these sources are roofing contractors or agencies, so treat specific percentages as marketing-grade; the direction is consistent.
  • Hail-prone states can be stricter: reports describe 10-year rules for asphalt roofs in Colorado, with State Farm using actual-cash-value roof endorsements and Nationwide a limited roof endorsement at 11 years.
  • Florida law (as summarized by roofing contractors citing statute 627.7011) bars refusing or non-renewing a policy solely because of roof age when the roof is under 15 years, and from 15 years lets insurers require an inspection; an inspection showing at least 5 years of remaining life defeats an age-only refusal. Verify the current statute.
  • Actual cash value deducts depreciation. Contractor sources illustrate a 15-year-old $20,000 roof paying about $5,000; reports that depreciation reduces payouts 40% to 60% are promotional. The tool depreciates the roof in a straight line over its expected life (asphalt 20 years, tile 35, metal 45, all assumptions) and shows replacement-cost and actual-cash-value outcomes side by side.

7. Flood and earthquake

  • Standard homeowners policies do not cover flood. The National Flood Insurance Program offers up to $250,000 for a home and $100,000 for contents (contents cover must be requested), and premiums are set by the program, not the insurer. Sources give average premiums from about $500 to $1,200 a year; costs vary with zone and coverage.
  • There is normally a 30-day waiting period before NFIP cover takes effect.
  • Lenders must require flood insurance for federally backed mortgages on property in a Special Flood Hazard Area. Higher limits are available from private flood insurers.
  • Earthquake is also excluded from standard policies and sold as an add-on, usually with a large deductible.
  • The FEMA flood zone and National Risk Index links, and the local address helper, give area evidence; they are not house-level assessments.

8. Keeping cover: non-renewal, residual markets and what to do

  • Florida: Citizens Property Insurance is the state-run insurer of last resort. One roofing source reports a 120-day written notice requirement for non-renewal with the reason stated; verify with the Office of Insurance Regulation.
  • California: the FAIR Plan is a shared pool of insurers. A broker summary says it covers named perils only (fire, lightning, smoke, internal explosion) with a $3 million cap per residential structure, so theft, liability and water damage may need a separate difference-in-conditions policy. California law also limits non-renewals in wildfire moratorium areas.
  • North Carolina: the Department of Insurance describes a separate wind policy through the state underwriting association for coastal homes.
  • Many other states run a FAIR Plan or similar. Ask the state insurance department.

If you are non-renewed or cannot find cover

  1. Get the notice in writing and ask for the reason.
  2. Compare at least three quotes, including an independent agent who can shop several insurers.
  3. Ask whether a roof inspection or repair, or a wind-mitigation inspection, could keep or restore eligibility.
  4. Keep continuous coverage; a lapse can trigger force-placed lender insurance.
  5. Use the insurer of last resort only when nothing else is available, and check what it does not cover.

  • A claim raises the price. One claim in five years pays about 16% more than a claim-free household ($3,961 vs. $3,417), two claims about 29% more (MoneyGeek, March 2026). An Insurance.com study by claim type puts one claim at about 16% for weather, 25% for water and 29% for fire. Claims stay on a CLUE report for five to seven years. Insurers differ widely after a claim, and a Consumer Reports survey found half of claimants saw no increase in the following years.
  • Implication: the tool's renewal and stress-test sections tell users when a small claim (below or near the deductible) is not worth filing, and to compare after any claim.

10. Architecture for a home insurance product

Modules

  1. Intake and enrichment: address to coordinates (Census geocoder), FEMA flood zone (National Flood Hazard Layer), tract-level hazard ratings (National Risk Index). Licensed property-level data (Guidewire HazardHub, ZestyAI) later, for anything that influences price.
  2. State rules engine: credit-use bans (CA, MD, MA), hurricane deductible rules and triggers (for example Florida), roof-age protections, non-renewal notice rules, producer licensing scope.
  3. Insurance-to-value engine: rebuild range from several sources; later a contractor-grade estimator or a licensed replacement-cost API.
  4. Pricing engine: published state curves plus sourced factors; quantile ranges; validated on real quotes before any "overpaying" verdict.
  5. Coverage-gap engine: storm deductible and roof terms, exclusions (flood, quake, sewer backup), endorsements, special limits, liability and umbrella.
  6. Stress-test simulator: deterministic payment logic from the user's own terms against published average and illustrative losses.
  7. Market-access engine: non-renewal signals (roof age, weather, wildfire, past non-renewal), state residual-market guidance.
  8. Explainability and audit: a decision receipt for every result, with each factor marked sourced or assumed, data dates and model version.

Governance and validation

  • Real-quote validation with the plan's thresholds: at least 80% of quotes inside the range and median error of 15% or less, by state, before any state's home results lose their "rough" label. The checker includes a Test tab that runs this on a CSV of quotes.
  • Independent review of any third-party hazard score before it influences a recommendation; decision-level accountability for the "switch" and "add cover" recommendations.
  • Bias screen across demographic groups and avoidance of ZIP-code proxies; state rules for credit.

Compliance

Recommending a policy or comparing quotes for compensation can trigger producer licensing in each state. The current design stays advisory, with no buying or binding. Insurance is regulated state by state, and AI-in-insurance guidance is still spreading state by state (NAIC bulletin adopted by about 25 states plus D.C. by mid-2026, according to sources found).

Design principles taken from the consultancy benchmark

  • Govern decisions, not just models (PwC): every recommendation carries a receipt.
  • Transparent models (KPMG, Oliver Wyman): assumptions and data dates are shown to the user.
  • Independent model review (Milliman): third-party scores get validated before use.
  • Geospatial enrichment (Cognizant): flood and hazard lookups by address.
  • Protection-gap framing (McKinsey): the product measures gaps, not only price.
  • Consumer-side independence: none of these firms serves consumers directly, so an independent, non-lead-selling checker is the differentiator.

12. Open items and recommended next steps

  1. Get real home quotes. Collect 50 to 100 identical-coverage quotes per pilot state and run them through the Test tab. This is the only way to know whether the home range and levels are right. The quote collection kit is ready: request sheets for Texas, Florida and California (60 profiles each, price column blank), the standard coverage to request, the collection protocol, and decision rules. The quotes themselves have not been collected: they need a licensed agent or the insurers' own systems.
  2. Choose a home-price source. The two published sources disagree by 25% to 60% in the states compared. License one dataset (or several) and document the choice.
  3. License replacement-cost and hazard data. A contractor-grade rebuild estimator and property-level scores would replace the three-estimate range and the area-level ratings.
  4. Live-check the address helper and confirm the hurricane, wildfire and flood rating field names.
  5. Verify state rules the tool summarizes from secondary sources (Florida roof and notice rules, hurricane deductible triggers by state) against the statutes or insurance department pages.
  6. Licensing advice before any purchase path, and a decision on whether the product stays advisory.
  7. More browser testing: Chromium (desktop and phone sizes) was tested for layout, printing and the copy button, and one phone-layout bug was fixed; Safari, Firefox and real phones were not tested.

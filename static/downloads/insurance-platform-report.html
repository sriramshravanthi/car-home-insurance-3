Summary

This is the strongest version I can build without real customer quotes, live services or legal input, and with browser testing limited to Chromium. It is a learning-grade tool for checking car and home insurance, with published benchmarks, tested against real insurer prices from one state, with built-in fairness and monitoring tools. It is not a quote engine and not advice.

What you have

  • The checker (insurance-price-check.html): car and home flows for all 50 states and D.C., a price estimate with a range and a percentile, coverage checks, ways to lower cost without creating a gap, a test bench for real quotes, and a Trust and fairness section.
  • This report, the home blueprint (deep dive on home), the quote collection kit with request sheets for Texas, Florida and California, and Oklahoma's official insurer samples as a tidy CSV.
  • An address helper (insurance_risk_service.py) that fills flood and hazard boxes from FEMA data.

Five findings worth knowing

  1. Insurers disagree far more than most people expect. In Oklahoma's official samples the highest insurer charged about 4.7 to 4.9 times the lowest for the same driver or home. A single "correct" price does not exist, so the tool shows a range and where your price sits among insurers.
  2. My first model was wrong, and testing found it. Against those official prices it was 12% to 51% low. It now sits within about 6% of the median insurer for adult drivers and about 10% for homes, after fixes to the cost of own-car cover, teen drivers, small houses and the width of the ranges.
  3. The biggest home risks are gaps, not price. Published rebuild costs run from $162 to $350 a square foot, one survey found three in four homeowners underinsured, a 2% hurricane deductible on a $560,000 house is $11,200, and an older roof paid at depreciated value can leave most of a $20,000 replacement to the owner.
  4. When prices rise, households cut protection. J.D. Power reports that financially constrained drivers, especially younger ones, more often reduce coverage or let policies lapse than shop. The checker now ranks ways to save that do not create a gap, and lists what not to cut.
  5. Regulators expect documented, tested, monitored models. The NAIC model bulletin, New York's Circular Letter No. 7 and Colorado's SB21-169 all point to governance, bias testing and documentation. The checker includes a model card, controls register, fairness screen and monitoring tool, and is honest about the two controls still missing (an accountable owner and an incident process).

What is not done. No real quotes for Texas, Florida or California have been tested; only Oklahoma's official samples, and some fixes used the same data, so the validation is partial. Home prices are the roughest number: a second published source is 25% to 60% lower in the states compared. The address helper has passed only offline tests. No real browser was available, so layout, printing and the copy button are untested on screen. There has been no legal review.

1. How to use it

  1. Open insurance-price-check.html from your computer (double-click). Nothing is uploaded or stored.
  2. Choose Car insurance or Home insurance and answer the steps. The form starts with an example so you can see how it works.
  3. Read the verdict, the range and the percentile, then the coverage sections and the stress test, and use "Take this to an agent".
  4. To test the model, open Test with real quotes: press the Oklahoma button for the built-in official comparison, or build a request sheet and load filled quotes (see the collection kit).
  5. Open Trust and fairness for the model card and controls, the rules audit, the fairness screen and monitoring over time.
  6. Optional: run python insurance_risk_service.py, keep the checker open from disk, and use "Look up my address".

2. Market context

  • Car prices vary widely by state. NerdWallet's September 2026 analysis of public filings puts the national median full-coverage premium at $2,344 a year, from $1,148 in Wyoming to $4,296 in Louisiana, for a 35-year-old with a clean record and good credit.
  • Home prices vary more. Insurance.com's September 15, 2026 averages for $300,000 of cover range from $738 in Hawaii to $8,471 in Florida, with a national average of $2,872. Other published sources put the national figure as low as about $1,700, and are 25% to 60% lower than Insurance.com in the ten states compared.
  • Claim costs are at records. LexisNexis reports all-peril claim severity in 2025 up 25.9% from 2024 and 93.2% above 2019, with fire and lightning severity up 67.3% (largely the Los Angeles wildfires).
  • Access to cover is tightening in some places. California's FAIR Plan held about 668,000 policies with about $724 billion of exposure at a January 2026 hearing; Florida's Citizens plays the same insurer-of-last-resort role.
  • Households react to price. J.D. Power's second-quarter 2026 report shows auto shopping at 12.6% and switching at 4.5%, home shopping at 7.1% and switching at 2.5%, and a median premium above $3,200 among auto switchers. Two thirds of consumers use AI in researching coverage; few follow the guidance exactly, and more than a third changed a policy based on it. That last point is why the tool tells users to verify and to talk to an agent.

3. Regulation and governance

Rules the tool respects

  • Credit. Banned for car pricing in California, Hawaii, Massachusetts and Michigan, and for home pricing in California, Maryland and Massachusetts; the tool ignores credit there. Oregon and Utah limit its use for car pricing.
  • Licensing. Recommending a policy or comparing quotes for compensation can trigger producer licensing in each state. The tool is advisory: it does not sell, bind or compare live quotes. Get counsel's view before adding a purchase path.
  • State minimum liability. Checked against official state sources for Texas, Florida, New York, Michigan, Pennsylvania, Washington, Massachusetts, Hawaii, Ohio and North Carolina; California's statute is identified and Illinois is partly checked; other states come from consumer guides and are labelled.

AI and fairness expectations

  • The NAIC model bulletin asks insurers to keep a governance and risk-management framework so that models are documented, tested and validated, including for unfair discrimination. Sources put adoption at about 25 states and D.C. by mid-2026, with more than 20 having adopted or proposed guidance by late 2025.
  • New York's Insurance Circular Letter No. 7 (2024) requires insurers to be able to show that external data and AI systems do not proxy for protected classes or produce disproportionate adverse effects, and to document their testing methods.
  • Colorado's SB21-169 bars unfair discrimination through external data and algorithms and requires governance and testing; its definition turns on a disproportionately negative outcome that exceeds the reasonable correlation to the underlying insurance risk.
  • Tests widely used include the adverse impact ratio and regression-based checks, with a known caution: when group labels are inferred from names or addresses, measurement error can distort the results.

These rules apply to insurers. This checker is not an insurer and its outputs do not set anyone's price, but the same practices (documentation, testing, monitoring) are the right standard for a tool people rely on.

Range and percentile

The range is the middle half of expected insurer prices: about x0.74 to x1.35 for cars and x0.73 to x1.37 for homes. It comes from how much insurers differ for identical customers in Oklahoma's official samples (log standard deviation 0.37 to 0.43), plus an assumed 0.20 for the tool's own error. The percentile applies the same spread to say how your price compares with what insurers would charge someone like you. Both are rough and based on one state.

6. Coverage checks

Car

  • Own-car cover suggested from car value, loan status and local hazards, with the price effect of changing it.
  • Liability check: the state legal minimum, your limits, and a suggested level from what you could lose. Legal is not the same as safe.
  • Uninsured and underinsured drivers: whether your state requires the cover (New York, Illinois, Massachusetts, North Carolina, Connecticut, Kansas and Maine per the sources found) or only requires an offer (Pennsylvania, Hawaii, Texas).
  • No-fault states (12): personal injury protection and the lawsuit threshold in plain language. SR-22 and FR-44 after a DUI. Telematics as a what-if with its risks.

Home

  • Rebuild cost: three published estimates side by side with your shortfall against each, and the coinsurance warning below 80%.
  • Storm deductibles and roof: the dollar amount of a percentage deductible on your limit, against the average wind or hail claim of about $13,500; the roof paid at replacement cost versus actual cash value; roof-age eligibility notes, including Florida's roof-age rule as summarized by roofing contractors (verify the statute).
  • Policy stress test: what your policy would pay, using your own terms, in 11 situations from a fire to a large lawsuit, using published average claim sizes (fire $83,991, liability $31,690, water $13,954, wind and hail $13,511, theft $5,024) and clearly labelled illustrative losses.
  • Endorsements and special limits (ordinance or law, extended replacement cost, water backup, contents valuation, umbrella) and market access, including the FAIR Plan and Citizens facts and what to do if non-renewed.
  • Flood and earthquake are excluded from standard policies; NFIP offers up to $250,000 for a home and $100,000 for contents after a typical 30-day wait, and lenders must require flood cover for federally backed mortgages in a Special Flood Hazard Area.

Both

  • A protection-gap score (car: liability, uninsured drivers, own-car cover, deductible; home: eight weighted items), ways to lower cost without creating a gap with the things not to cut, a renewal check with deductible break-even, a quote comparison that caps the score of thin-coverage quotes, a claim-readiness checklist, and a decision receipt for every result.

8. Trust tools

  • Model card: intended use, what it is not for, how it works, validation numbers computed live, limitations, the controls register with honest status, change log, and where to complain.
  • Rules audit: how much each input moves the estimate and whether the multiple is sourced or assumed; which inputs are used (state, age, driving record, claims, credit where allowed, property facts) and which are not (ZIP code, gender, marital status, occupation, income, education, race, religion, national origin); and the non-price decisions that depend on inputs.
  • Fairness screen for your own data: adverse impact ratios (flag below 0.80) for favorable outcomes or cost ratios (flag above 1.25) for prices, a significance test, small-group warnings, and a proxy screen (correlation ratio or Cramer's V) showing which columns could stand in for group membership. A flag prompts investigation; it is not a finding of discrimination.
  • Monitoring for your own data: monthly bias, range coverage and median error, a population stability index for the mix of inputs, and alerts (bias above 10%, coverage below 80%, error above 15%, mix shift above 0.25).
  • Accessibility: an automated scan (axe-core) of the main screens found and I fixed three problems (an unlabelled summary box, empty table headers, and content outside landmarks); it now reports none on six screens. All colour pairs meet the WCAG AA text-contrast ratio of 4.5 in light and dark themes, checked by calculation. Not done: screen-reader, keyboard-only and real-device testing.

The fairness screen and monitoring need data the tool does not have. They are ready to run when quotes or outcomes exist; until then, the model card records that testing has not been done on real outcomes.

10. Limits and next steps

Limits, in order of importance

  1. No real-quote validation in the pilot states. Oklahoma's official samples are the only real per-insurer data tested, and the fit is partly in-sample.
  2. Home prices are one source's view. Another published source is 25% to 60% lower in the states compared.
  3. State-level only. City moved the same profile's price by up to about 16% (car) and 22% (home) within Oklahoma; the tool cannot see your address.
  4. The percentile borrows Oklahoma's insurer spread for every state.
  5. Untested in practice: the address helper against live services; the tool in Safari, Firefox and on real phones; screen-reader use. (Chromium desktop and phone-size layout, printing and copy were tested.)
  6. No owner, no incident process, no legal review; the two controls marked "not built" in the model card.
